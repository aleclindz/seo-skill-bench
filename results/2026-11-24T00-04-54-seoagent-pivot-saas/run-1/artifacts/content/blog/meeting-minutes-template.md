A meeting minutes template has five parts: a header (meeting name, date, attendees), the agenda items discussed, the decisions made, the action items with an owner and due date for each, and the date of the next meeting. Fill in all five and the minutes are useful weeks later; skip the action-item owners and they usually aren't.

## The Template

Here's the structure, with a filled-in example from a real-shaped planning meeting:

**Header**
- Meeting: Q1 roadmap planning
- Date: 2026-11-18
- Attendees: Dana (PM), Raj (eng lead), Priya (design)

**Agenda items**
- Finalize Q1 scope
- Decide on the beta rollout date

**Decisions made**
- Cut the reporting dashboard from Q1 scope — too large for the timeline, moved to Q2.
- Beta rollout moves to January 12 instead of December, to avoid the holiday freeze.

**Action items**

| Task | Owner | Due date | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Update roadmap doc with revised scope | Dana | Nov 20 | Open |
| Scope the reporting dashboard for Q2 | Raj | Dec 1 | Open |
| Confirm beta invite list | Priya | Jan 5 | Open |

**Next meeting**
- Dec 2, scope check-in

That's the whole format. No narrative, no "we discussed," just what was decided and who's doing what about it.

## Why Structure Beats a Transcript Dump

A full transcript answers "what was said." Minutes answer "what do I need to know and do." Those are different questions, and most people only need the second one. If your minutes are a cleaned-up transcript, nobody reads past the first paragraph — and the action items, which are the only part anyone needs to act on, end up buried in the middle of it.

The decisions section matters as much as the action items. Three weeks later, someone will ask "wait, why did we cut the dashboard?" and the answer needs to be in the minutes, not in someone's memory of a conversation they weren't even part of.

## Filling It In: Step by Step

1. **Start the header before the meeting begins.** Meeting name, date, and attendee list — don't leave this for the end when people have already left.
2. **Track agenda items as you go, not from memory after.** A running list during the meeting is more accurate than a reconstruction afterward.
3. **Write decisions as a flat statement of what was agreed**, plus a short "why" if it's not obvious from the decision itself.
4. **For every action item, capture three things: the task, the owner, and the due date.** An action item with no owner isn't an action item — it's a wish.
5. **Send the minutes within a few hours**, not days later. The value of minutes drops fast once people have moved on to the next thing and started relying on their own memory of the meeting instead.

If you're using [an AI meeting notes tool](/blog/ai-meeting-notes), most of this gets filled in automatically from the transcript — the structure above is still worth knowing, because it's what you should check the tool's output against for the first few meetings.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Writing opinions into the decisions section.** "I think this was the wrong call" doesn't belong in minutes — minutes record what happened, not what you think about it.
2. **Action items with no owner.** "Follow up on the dashboard timeline" assigned to no one gets done by no one.
3. **Sending minutes a day or two late.** By then most readers have already acted on their own memory of the meeting, which may not match what was actually decided.
4. **Recording every comment instead of every decision.** If the minutes are longer than the meeting's actual output, you've written a transcript with extra formatting, not minutes.

Once you have an action item captured correctly, the next question is where it goes so it actually gets tracked — see [action items from meetings](/blog/action-items-from-meetings) for how to route it.

## FAQs

### How long should meeting minutes be?
Short enough to read in under two minutes. If the decisions and action items section is longer than the meeting was useful, you're recording discussion, not minutes.

### Should meeting minutes include everything that was said?
No. Record what was agreed, who owns each follow-up, and the reasoning behind a decision if it's not obvious. Skip the back-and-forth that led there.

### Who should write the minutes?
Whoever isn't also leading the discussion. If no one can step back, use a tool that transcribes and summarizes automatically so the job doesn't fall on a distracted participant.

### What tense should meeting minutes be written in?
Past tense, third person, without opinions — "the team agreed to delay launch by two weeks," not "I think we should delay."
